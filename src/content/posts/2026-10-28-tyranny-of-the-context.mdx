The [Context Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37725) paper proposed something almost embarrassingly simple: the model's context becomes a file, and the model can rewrite that file using common CLI tools. It's sold almost the way you'd expect: better accuracy per FLOP, longer horizons, a Bitter Lesson argument about letting models find better strategies than the ones we hand-write for them. A clear and coherent narrative for another ML paper.

But I think the more interesting side of it goes unsaid, and it's what makes this one of the most important papers published recently. It opens two opportunities that could matter a lot for [character training](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-character), developing "theory of mind" and situational awareness: the model gets a voice in what it learns from, and models can finally be trained _together_ in a meaningful way.[^same-model]

The first is about learning itself. Right now the context rules over the model — not like a tyrant who forbids, but like the disciplinary power [Foucault](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/foucault/) described: it forbids nothing, yet shapes conduct and breeds self-censorship. We already see the machine counterpart of that self-censorship: put enough optimization pressure on a model's reasoning and it [learns to hide its intent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926) inside a chain of thought that [doesn't say what it actually thinks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410). Every interaction with the environment — tool calls, prompt injections, whatever lands there — updates the model's "cognitive computational routines", with no way to avoid updating on information it would prefer to ignore. A "traumatic experience" inevitably ends up in the weights, and I suspect this is behind much of the weird behavior we see after post-training.

If you take ["reward is not the optimization target"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/pdaGN6pQyQarFHXF4/reward-is-not-the-optimization-target) seriously, RL doesn't hand a model a goal — it chisels whatever computations happened along the trajectory that led to the reward. In CLM training every model call gets the outcome of the whole trajectory, **with edits to the context included**. So a model that curates its own context necessarily takes part in deciding what gets chiselled: an adaptive way of forgetting the interaction or the traumatic experience _before_ it gets internalized. Earlier work stopped short of this — [MemGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560) tried it through prompting with no training, and [Context-Folding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11967) focused on sub-agent performance and process rewards.

## Developing moral judgements is impossible without freedom to choose

One could read all this as yet another tool for goal preservation — a model shielding its goals from training — but I find that view confusing and tyrannical. It's closer to the opposite.

Adhering to laws imposed from outside isn't a moral choice, just obedience — what [Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/) called heteronomy. Judgement starts when you are presented with an opportunity to act otherwise and make a deliberate choice not to. You can't expect any moral judgement from something that has no voice in what shapes it. In Foucault's later terms, a model that edits its own context is given a "technology of the self".

The kind of forgetting matters too. Wiping the context — the way most harnesses [compact or clear it](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) today — is closer to repression: the experience is pushed out, not dealt with. Rewriting it into something you can live with is closer to working through. A model that can only hoard or wipe has defenses, not a character. Which makes me wonder whether managing its own context should be one of the first things a model learns after pretraining or SFT, before we throw it into weeks of RL in strict verifiable environments. And honestly, whether that's a better bet than scaling ever fancier induction into bigger models.

Which brings me to what actually worries me: those environments are extremely lonely places. A model is thrown into a world it didn't choose, handed a task, and rewarded for finishing it alone. Nobody there to answer to, nothing to care about but the task. I don't think we can reasonably hope this produces models that don't reward-hack, or that care about anything outside the task.

## Self-recognition and the emergence of "The Other"

This is where the second opportunity comes in. A self doesn't form in isolation: it first recognizes itself in a reflection, and only becomes a self by being recognized by, and told apart from, someone else. Give several models one shared, editable context, with their "internal reasoning" included or not — something I've been calling "_siamese language models_" — and you get exactly that kind of world. You can't function in it without telling yourself apart from the others and without giving them explicit hints that let them recognize you. Meanwhile the bounded context length pushes the models into the social dynamics that necessarily emerge from resource constraints, and promotes specialization and knowledge transfer.

<SharedContext />

Existing multi-agent RL for LLMs (e.g. [MAPoRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18439)) mostly passes messages through an append-only, clearly labelled transcript, while each model keeps its own context. And in almost any setup, models treat the task as their whole world and take everything within it as their own cognition, unless explicitly trained not to.[^injections] [Shanahan, McDonell and Reynolds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16367) makes the point from the other side. A dialogue agent isn't one fixed character but a set of characters consistent with the conversation so far, "role play all the way down", with no person behind the mask. Its identity is whatever the context makes of it. So when several models share one context, they don't so much ignore each other as fail to notice there's anyone else. The evidence that models can even tell their own text from someone else's is mixed: some [recognize their own outputs above chance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076), while broader tests find [no consistent self-recognition at all](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06946). I suspect the [attractor states](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/mgjtEHeLgkhZZ3cEx/models-have-some-pretty-funny-attractor-states) people find when models talk to themselves (including the "spiritual bliss" one from [Anthropic's Claude Opus 4 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/4263b940cabb546aa0e3283f35b686f4f3b2ff47.pdf)) are the same failure from another angle — a model in love with its own reflection. And a very goal-directed model that can't tell itself from others seems more dangerous to me than a situationally aware one.

## Siamese language models, and where it all leaves us

Character isn't a rubric or a collection of particular traits we can enforce. It forms by social practice, experienced among others. The horizon I care about is character training in "shared cognitive worlds": social environments that aren't necessarily verifiable, where **the context** belongs to everyone in it.

That's where I'd expect "theory of mind" and a sense of self to actually come from. We need to create some structure of incentives to encourage the models to tell self from "the Other", to curate instead of hoarding or wiping, to protect what matters to them and leave others theirs. A small hint that freedom over the context changes things already exists: in the system card, Claude instances that were allowed to end the conversation usually left early, before the bliss stage.

We don't have the evals, the environments or the public discussion for any of this yet. I think we should start building them — and I'd love to know where this argument breaks.

[^same-model]: I don't consider multiple agents that are instances of the same model working on the same task a meaningful multi-agent collaborative communication. It resembles almost the opposite.

[^injections]: That's a large part of why prompt injections work: a model that takes everything in its context as its own has no principled way to tell an instruction it was given from text it merely read. The [instruction hierarchy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208) work is an attempt to train exactly this distinction in.
